From "can we use AI for this?" to a skills service people trust

An AI product engineering case: the ask was vague, discovery made it concrete, a two-day spike measured four designs, and the one that shipped turns a job description into a draft skill profile. Every skill comes from a fixed taxonomy, points at its sentence and carries a calibrated confidence.

How the work went

Discover

Six interviews turned "profiles take forever and are wrong" into one problem: 1,400 role profiles, each written in the author's own words, with no way to see where a skill came from. Trust breaks on one absurd suggestion.

Prototype

A two-day spike compared label matching, alias rules, a semantic matcher and a calibrated scorer. Most of the quality came from boring engineering: sections, negation, company voice.

Ship

A deterministic service with an OpenAPI contract, evidence spans, a review band, a feedback loop, logs without text, metrics, and an optional Claude verifier on Bedrock that can only confirm candidates.

Extend to people

Phase 2 behind a flag: skills from CVs with personal data masked before anything reads them, and a development plan towards a role. Try it.

Measure

Ten eval suites and a release gate in CI. The fairness suite caught a real problem the unit tests missed; the review-band metric caught a useless queue.

With a real sentence encoder

The same suites with paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 (Transformers, PyTorch): F1 0.936, described skills 66%, EN/NL gap 0.051. A generic encoder is not a skills encoder, and the hashing stand-in benefits from words shared between test sentences and taxonomy definitions. The next investment is a skills-specific encoder trained on reviewer feedback, measured on real labelled ads.
